Why most AI pilots stall, and how to make yours ship

The demo works, everyone is impressed, and three months later nobody uses it. The reasons are rarely about the model.

Iona Hirsch13 October 2026 · 5 min read

Building an AI prototype has never been easier. Getting one into daily use is still hard, and the gap between the two is where most of the budget disappears. When I look at pilots that quietly faded out, the same five causes come up.

1. Nobody measured the baseline

If you do not know how long the task takes today, or how often it goes wrong, you cannot show that the new version is better. The pilot ends on a feeling rather than a number, and feelings lose budget arguments.

Instead: before building anything, time and grade the current process on a sample of real cases. That sample becomes your test set.

2. It lived outside the workflow

A separate tab, a new login, a tool people have to remember to open. Busy teams fall back on habit within a week.

Instead: put the pilot where the work already happens: the help desk, the CRM, the shared inbox, the team chat.

3. Nobody owned it

Pilots run by an innovation team or an outside vendor often have no home once the project ends. Everyone liked it; no one was responsible for it.

Instead: name an owner inside the team that will use it, from the start, and make the result part of their goals.

4. It was judged on the best case

Demos are built on clean, typical examples. Real work is messier: odd formats, missing fields, the angry customer who writes in capitals. A pilot that shines on the easy cases and fails on the hard ones loses the team’s trust quickly.

Instead: test on a representative sample, messy cases included, and agree in advance what score counts as good enough.

5. The scope kept growing

Once the first version works, the requests start: can it also handle refunds, write the report, talk to the other system? Each addition is small. Together they mean the pilot never finishes.

Instead: one task, one team, a fixed time box. Expand only after the first version is in daily use.

A checklist for pilots that ship

  • The current process is measured on a real sample.
  • The bar for success is agreed before building starts.
  • It runs inside a tool the team already uses.
  • A named owner in that team is responsible for it.
  • It has a fixed time box and a decision date: ship, adjust or stop.

A pilot that clears all five may still fail, but you will know why, and you will know quickly. If you have one that is stuck, I am happy to take a look.
